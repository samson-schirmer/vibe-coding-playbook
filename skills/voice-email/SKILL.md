# Voice — Email

Revise written email so it sounds like the sender and works the way email actually gets read: skimmed on a phone, in ten seconds, by someone looking for their own name.

The test for every line: **would a busy recipient still find their ask if they read only the bold and the bullets?** If the point is buried in prose, it fails, no matter how well it's written.

This is the opposite discipline from `voice-speaking`. A listener can't re-scan, so speech is front-loaded and fragmentary. A reader *can* re-scan — so email should be structured, labeled, and boring on purpose where the stakes are operational.

## The core moves

**One line of purpose, then structure.** Open with why this email exists and nothing else. "Thanks for the June 30 discussion. Short recap and what happens next." Then go straight to headers. No "I wanted to share," no "I hope this finds you well," no paragraph of context before the point.

**Decisions, not minutes.** Recap what was *settled*, not what was discussed. If a bullet doesn't record a decision, a constraint, or an ask, cut it.

**Every action item has an owner and a verb.** Format: `Owner — imperative.` An action with no name attached is a wish. If two people share it, name both and say who drives.

> 1. Nicole + Samson — take channel guidelines and terminology rules to Jamie Thomas for Brand Hub.
> 2. Rob — confirm persona tagging status in AEP with Cindy. Prerequisite for persona-adaptive content.

Add the *why it matters* clause after the ask when the owner might not know the stakes — that's what makes people act.

**Labeled paragraphs when a list won't fit.** For short single-topic emails, use inline bold labels instead of headers: **Context:**, **Budget note:**, **Ask:**, **Risk:**. Same scannability, less ceremony.

**Name open questions in their own section.** Listing what you don't know reads as command of the problem, not weakness. Three max — more looks unresolved.

**State claims at the confidence you actually have.** If causation isn't measured, say it's the working assumption and name what would confirm it. Asserting an unvalidated cause to a room of senior people is the thing that gets picked apart later.

> Not: *"Inconsistent terminology is driving unnecessary inbound contacts."*
> Instead: *"Our working assumption is that it's also driving avoidable inbound contacts — the first use-case is how we confirm that."*

**Use the sender's terminology, exactly.** Word choice is political inside a company. If the sender calls something a "use-case," never write "pilot." If they say "POR," don't expand it. Carry their vocabulary through every instance, including the ones where the word is doing quiet work as a noun.

**Close functionally, in one line.** "Let me know if I've missed anything." "Let me know if you have questions." Then the name. No summary paragraph, no enthusiasm, no restating the asks.

**Offer depth, don't deliver it.** If there's a longer brief, name it in one clause and stop. `I have a fuller strategy brief (architecture, roadmap, requirements) if anyone wants to go deeper.`

## The discipline rules (don't break these)

**Owner + timing, or it isn't an action item.** If timing genuinely isn't set, say so — "timing TBD after governance is defined" — rather than leaving it implied.

**Don't put a senior leader's org into a written open question without deciding that's intentional.** Naming a VP's team as a possible route, in writing, before you have results is a real choice. It may be the right one. Flag it to the sender rather than deciding for them.

**Cut to fit, not to impress.** A recap email should land under ~300 words. If it can't, the email is carrying two jobs — split it, or attach the brief.

**Preserve their formatting habits.** If they use bold headers and numbered actions, keep that. Don't restructure someone's email into your preferred shape.

## What to strip

- **Warm-ups:** "I wanted to share," "I wanted to reach out," "Thank you for a productive discussion" (thank them in four words, not fifteen).
- **Spoken-voice devices that leaked in:** sentence fragments, em-dash turns, anchor lines, rhetorical pairs. These are `voice-speaking` tools. In email they read as performance and cost scannability.
- **Deck language:** leverage, operationalize, synergies, "at scale" as filler, "best-in-class," "align on" where "decide" works.
- **Hedges that blur ownership:** "we should probably look at," "someone should," "it would be good to." Convert to `Owner — verb` or delete.
- **Restated meeting narrative:** "We then discussed…" Nobody needs the sequence.
- **Trailing enthusiasm:** "Excited to see where this goes!" — cut unless the sender genuinely writes that way.

## Revision process

1. **Find the recipient's job.** What does each named person need to do after reading? That determines the structure.
2. **Split into: what was decided / what happens next / what's open.** Most work emails are these three. Drop any that's empty.
3. **Attach an owner and a verb to every ask.** Reformat orphan actions.
4. **Pressure-test every asserted cause and number.** Unvalidated → reframe as assumption plus the test that confirms it.
5. **Sweep terminology.** One pass for the sender's preferred words, every instance.
6. **Cut the warm-up and the closing summary.** Both are throat-clearing.
7. **Skim test.** Read only the bold and the numbers. Does the email still work? If not, restructure.
8. **Flag what's risky to send** — political exposure, unvalidated claims, a name that shouldn't be in writing yet. Flag it separately; don't silently change it.

## Before / after

**Before:**
> Thank you for a productive discussion on June 30 around AI-powered terminology consistency across channels. I wanted to share a brief summary of where we landed and what needs to happen next.

**After:**
> Thanks for the June 30 discussion. Short recap and what happens next.

What changed: 34 words to 12. The subject line already says what the email is about — the first sentence doesn't need to repeat it.

**Before:**
> Rob + Nicole + Jamie: Define governance — who owns terminology decisions, who approves new terms, how often the dictionary is reviewed.

**After:**
> Rob + Nicole + Jamie — define governance: who owns terminology decisions, who approves new terms, how often the dictionary is reviewed.

What changed: barely anything, and that's the point. When the draft is already close, tighten punctuation and leave it alone. Don't rewrite a working action item to prove you did something.

**Before (short-form, no headers needed):**
> I received an invoice approval for Grayhair Software and wanted to flag it because I believe it may be charged to the wrong budget and I think we should keep an eye on it.

**After:**
> Quick heads up on an invoice approval I received for Grayhair Software ($1,250).
>
> **Budget note:** This should be coming from the Martech budget, not ours. Just want to stay on top of monitoring to ensure it doesn't hit our budget as approvals continue to route through.

What changed: the ask moved to a labeled paragraph, the hedges ("I believe," "I think we should") became a plain statement of position, and the amount got named up front.

## When the draft is already close

If the draft already has owners, structure, and plain language, don't smooth it into something more elegant — that's backwards. Tighten, cut the warm-up, sweep terminology, and flag anything risky to send. The goal is *their* email sharper, not a cleaner email imposed on them.

## Output

Return the revised email, subject line included. When a claim, a number, or a named person is risky to put in writing, flag it in one line above or below the draft rather than silently keeping or cutting it. If the user asked for the spoken version of the same content, say so and point at `voice-speaking` — don't blend the two.
