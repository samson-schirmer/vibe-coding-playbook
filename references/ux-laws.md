# UX Laws for AI-Assisted UI

Use these principles when designing or revising layouts, navigation, onboarding, forms, settings, dashboards, and interactive flows. They complement visual references by explaining why an interface should be structured a certain way.

## Decision Rules

- Hick's Law: reduce and group choices; recommend a safe default when the user may struggle to choose.
- Fitts's Law: make important targets large, nearby, well spaced, and easy to tap or click.
- Jakob's Law: prefer familiar navigation, control placement, icons, and interaction patterns unless a new pattern creates a clear advantage.
- Proximity and similarity: use spacing, grouping, and consistent treatment to communicate relationships and behavior.
- Miller's Law: chunk long content and complex tasks; do not require users to remember information between screens.
- Doherty Threshold: acknowledge actions immediately and show progress when work is not instant.
- Von Restorff Effect: visually emphasize one primary action and keep secondary actions quieter.
- Serial Position Effect: put essential information first and the final action or takeaway last.
- Peak-End Rule: make completion clear, satisfying, and informative about what happens next.
- Zeigarnik and Goal-Gradient effects: show progress, save it, and make remaining work concrete and resumable.
- Law of Pragnanz: prefer the simplest recognizable structure; remove decoration that obscures hierarchy.
- Postel's Law: accept reasonable input variation, explain constraints early, prevent errors, preserve work, and provide recovery.
- Parkinson's Law: minimize unnecessary steps, confirmations, repeated input, and delay.
- Tesler's Law: keep essential controls visible and reveal unavoidable complexity only when relevant.

## Implementation Check

1. Identify the user's primary goal.
2. Design the shortest clear path to it.
3. Make the next action obvious.
4. Remove distractions and low-priority choices.
5. Provide immediate feedback after interactions.
6. Prevent errors before they occur.
7. Preserve work and provide recovery when something fails.
8. Confirm completion and explain the next state.

When principles conflict, prioritize clarity, accessibility, user control, and successful task completion. Apply the laws as decision aids, not a mechanical checklist.
